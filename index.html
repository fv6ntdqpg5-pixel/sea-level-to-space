Three patterns behind the numbers

The air has no top edge. It fades.

Pressure is the weight of the air stacked above you. Climb, and there is less above: the pressure is down to half by 18,000 ft and falls roughly tenfold with every 10 miles (16 km). On this scale each gridline is ten times thinner than the one above it, and the fade is close to a straight line across the chart. Higher up it keeps fading, more slowly, and never reaches a hard zero.

Pressure, % of sea level

Gravity weakens with the square of distance

The line is the inverse-square law, drawn from surface gravity and Earth's radius. Each dot is the pull needed to hold that object on its orbit, worked out from its distance and lap time. They fall on the line. The Moon sits 1% above it because the Moon's own mass adds to the pull. Ham radio operators time the Moon's distance by echo (about 2.5 seconds), and the tilt of any satellite dish gives the geostationary distance. The amber stretch is the whole range of the slider above.

Earth's pull, % of sea level

Above 60 miles (100 km) the gases sort by weight

Lower down, wind and turbulence keep the mix the same everywhere: 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen. Higher up there is too little air to stir, so each gas settles by its own weight, and sunlight splits O₂ into single atoms. The heaviest (argon, O₂, then N₂) thin out fastest. Lighter oxygen atoms take over from about 110 miles (180 km) and helium from about 400 miles (650 km). Hydrogen takes over higher still, beyond this chart. Gas centrifuges use the same effect to separate uranium isotopes by weight. The aurora's colours largely reflect this layering: green and red from oxygen atoms, pink from nitrogen at the lower fringe.

Check it yourself

Each of these is a number the physics commits to in advance. The first three show that air is held down by its own weight and needs no lid. They would come out the same on any world with a downward pull, so they do not settle the shape of the Earth. The last three are the ones that tell a turning globe from a flat plane.

Checks on the air column

Carry a barometer app upstairs

Many phones have a pressure sensor. Note the reading on the ground floor and again ten storeys up. Then try it in a closed room, floor to ceiling.

Predicted: about 3.6 hPa lower per 100 ft of climb near sea level. Pilots use 1 inch of mercury per 1,000 ft. In a closed room, about 0.12 hPa lower per metre, with no help from the walls.

Boil water on a mountain

Lower pressure lets water boil cooler. Put a thermometer in a rolling boil at two different elevations. It is why food packages carry high-altitude directions.

Predicted: about 1 °F lower for every 500 ft climbed. About 202 °F (95 °C) in Denver and 160 °F (71 °C) on Everest's summit.

Watch a weather balloon

The National Weather Service normally launches them twice a day from about 90 sites. Hobbyists fly cameras on the same kind of balloon and film it swelling as the outside pressure falls.

Predicted: 4.5 to 5 times its launch width when it bursts above 100,000 ft, where pressure is under 1% of sea level. Weather Service balloons go from about 5 ft to 20 or 25 ft.

Checks that tell a spinning globe from a flat plane

Time and measure the space station

Find a pass with a tracker such as Overhead Now. Two people 100 miles or more apart who photograph the same pass against the stars can work out its height from the shift. Radio amateurs hear its 145.800 MHz signal slide by about 3 kHz each way as it passes, which gives its speed.

Predicted: about 250 miles up, visible for 4 to 6 minutes on a good pass. One lap later, 93 minutes on, its track has moved 23° of longitude west, roughly 1,300 miles at mid-latitudes, because the ground turned under the orbit.

Watch a Foucault pendulum

Many science museums have one. The floor turns under the swing. Ship and aircraft gyroscopes sense the same turn, and so did the ring laser gyroscope run by flat-earth investigators in the 2018 film Behind the Curve.

Predicted: 15° per hour times the sine of the latitude. 11.3° per hour in Paris, zero on the equator, reversed south of it. A flat spinning disc would give the same rate everywhere.

Weigh the same mass in two places

Carry a 500 g calibration weight and a 0.01 g scale north or south by 15° of latitude, say Florida to the Great Lakes. Let the scale warm up, keep the room temperature and elevation similar, and do not recalibrate.

Predicted: about 0.7 g heavier at the northern end. From equator to pole the difference is 0.5% (9.780 against 9.832 m/s²), from the spin plus the bulge it causes.

Common objections, with numbers

"Gas needs a container. That is the second law."

A container is one way to hold gas. Weight is another. Every layer of air is squeezed by the air stacked above it and held down by gravity, so pressure eases off gradually with height. Nowhere in the open atmosphere does full-pressure air sit next to a vacuum. A gas spreads out evenly only where nothing pulls on it. Under a steady pull it settles into this fade. You can see it inside a closed room, where the walls play no part: a phone barometer reads about 0.12 hPa lower for each metre you lift it. A gas centrifuge shows it in a machine, with gas pressed against the spinning wall, near-vacuum at the centre and no barrier between.

"Gas expands to fill a vacuum, so the air would rush off into space"

Gas spreads until something balances its push. In a jar the walls do that. In the open air the weight of each layer does. To leave Earth for good a molecule must reach about 11 km/s (25,000 mph), and a typical nitrogen molecule moves at about 0.5 km/s. Only hydrogen and helium, the lightest gases, get away from the very top, at roughly 3 kg and 50 g a second. That is about 100,000 tonnes a year from an atmosphere of 5 million billion tonnes. The leak is why helium is only 5 parts per million of the air even though rocks release it constantly.

"It is density and buoyancy. No gravity needed."

Density has no direction. Something has to decide which way is down and push the denser material that way. That push is weight: the buoyant force on an object equals the weight of the fluid it displaces. Take weight away and buoyancy stops. Drop a leaking water bottle and the leak stops while it falls. In a vacuum chamber, with no air to float in, a feather and a coin fall together. In a braking car a helium balloon swings backward, because the air piles toward the front.

"We would feel 1,000 mph, and 67,000 mph around the Sun"

You feel changes in speed and direction, and what matters is how sharp the turn is. A playground merry-go-round at 10 turns a minute turns about 14,000 times faster than the Earth. For someone standing still, the spin's only effect is a slight outward lift: about a third of one percent of gravity at the equator and zero at the poles. Gravity holds the oceans about 290 times harder than the spin flings them. The path around the Sun bends even more gently, 0.06% of gravity, and everything on Earth follows it together, so nothing registers. Moving things also get a gentle sideways push from the spin. It turns the Foucault pendulum and makes hurricanes rotate opposite ways north and south of the equator.

"The air would be left behind as a 1,000 mph wind"

Nothing has to drag the air along. It is already moving with the ground and nothing stops it, like the air inside an airliner cabin at 550 mph. Empty space offers nothing to rub against. Friction with the surface only evens out differences, and the differences that remain are the winds.

"The thermosphere is 2,000°, so satellites would melt"

Temperature measures how fast molecules move. Heat delivered depends on how many of them hit you. Sparks from a grinder are well over 1,000 °F and bounce off your hand, while water at 212 °F scalds, because the water delivers far more heat. At the space station's height there are hundreds of billions of times fewer gas particles in a given volume than at sea level, so they deliver almost no heat. A satellite's temperature is set by sunlight and by what it radiates away.

"Gravity is 88% at the station, yet astronauts float"

Floating is what free fall feels like. The station and everyone in it are falling around the Earth together, moving sideways fast enough to keep missing it. Toss your phone with an accelerometer app running. It reads zero for the moment it is in the air, at full gravity, a few feet off the floor.

"Gravity holds the oceans but a butterfly can beat it"

At the surface gravity pulls on every kilogram equally hard: 9.8 newtons per kilogram. An ocean is heavy because it has an enormous mass. A butterfly weighs about half a gram, so the pull on it is about five thousandths of a newton, and its wings easily produce that much lift.

"Michelson and Morley proved the Earth is not moving"

That 1887 experiment looked for an aether wind from Earth's motion around the Sun and found none. It was not a test of rotation. Michelson tested rotation in 1925 with light sent around a 1.9 km loop of pipe in Illinois, and measured it: 0.230 of a fringe observed against 0.237 predicted. Ring laser gyroscopes repeat that measurement continuously today.

"These numbers all come from NASA and the government"

The Standard Atmosphere is a summary of measurements. The bottom 20 miles is measured twice a day by balloons launched in nearly every country, rivals included, and by hobbyists with ten-dollar pressure sensors. Every aircraft altimeter is built on it. The height of night-shining clouds was triangulated from the ground in the 1880s, and amateur camera networks triangulate meteor heights today. The checks above let you sample it yourself.

Atmosphere: U.S. Standard Atmosphere, 1976 (NOAA, NASA, U.S. Air Force), computed from its layer equations below 86 km and interpolated from its tables above. It describes average mid-latitude conditions. Real air varies with weather, season and latitude. Above 150 km the density swings about tenfold with the 11-year solar cycle, and the temperature there runs from roughly 900 to 2,000 °F (470 to 1,080 °C). The page shows the mid-cycle value.
